overview

The main theme of this session is to find a 1-Lipschitz map from a pair of pants to the complete 3-cusped surface.

Based on Thurston's cut-out argument, if we can obtain a cusped surface in this way, then by continuity, we can get a pair of pants of required boundary length. Since this map would be 1-Lipschitz, we complete the general case of Lemma 3.4.

Also, we mention the compromising in the construction of orthogonal foliations.

the 1-Lipschitz maps

the cup-out

First extend the pair of pants to a complete hyperbolic surface, this means gluing 3 funnels to the boundary circles. Choose a geodesic \a which intersects one boundary circle twice, and both ends go out to infinity to the same end of the surface. If there is a nearby geodesic \a′, then we can cut out the strip between \a,\a′ and glue the new boundary components.

the length of the seam

The resulting surface determines a pair of pants, where two boundary circles remain the same, and one boundary circle gets shorter.

After the cut, the upper boundary circle will not be a geodesic, the cuff 'goes upper', which means the map is not surjective.


This gets clearer in the right-angle hexagon picture. The length of seam 𝑠 satisfies
cosh⁡𝑠=cosh⁡𝑙12+cosh⁡𝑙22cosh⁡𝑙32sinh⁡𝑙22sinh⁡𝑙32.If \a,\a′ are parallel in the sense that \ol\a(𝑡)\a′(𝑡) is perpendicular to \a,\a′ for all 𝑡, then the seam remains a geodesic, say of length 𝑡, we also have cosh⁡𝑡=cosh⁡𝑙′12+cosh⁡𝑙22cosh⁡𝑙32sinh⁡𝑙22sinh⁡𝑙32.Now 𝑡 <𝑠, so 𝑙′1 <𝑙1, and in order to get a cusp, we can solve 𝑡 for 𝑙′1 =0, i.e. 𝑡 =\arccosh1+cosh⁡𝑙22cosh⁡𝑙32sinh⁡𝑙22sinh⁡𝑙32 >0.
(according to the sinh law, the other two seams tend to infinity as 𝑡 tends to this value)

The resulting surface consists of four trirectangles, each has one 0 angle

In such a trirectangle, we have sinh⁡𝑙22⋅sinh⁡𝑡1=1,which means 𝑡1=log⁡(1sinh⁡𝑙22+√1sinh2⁡𝑙22+1)=log⁡(1+cosh⁡𝑙22sinh⁡𝑙22)=log⁡(2cosh2⁡𝑙242sinh⁡𝑙24cosh⁡𝑙24)=log⁡(coth⁡𝑙24).Sum up to get 𝑡 =𝑡1 +𝑡2 =log⁡(coth⁡𝑙24coth⁡𝑙34).

By the way, we obtain an identity1+cosh⁡𝑎cosh⁡𝑏sinh⁡𝑎sinh⁡𝑏=cosh⁡log⁡(coth⁡𝑎2coth⁡𝑏2).

the gluing map

In order to get a 1-Lipschitz map, at least we should glue \a,\a′ via isometry. There are two possible ways of gluing.

Consider the universal cover. The left picture uses the Fermi coordinate, and the right picture uses horocycles

In the Fermi coordinates, we parameterize the fibers between \a,\a′ with the signed hyperbolic distance to the middle segment, say (𝑙,𝑠), where 𝑠 is the coordinate along the middle segment, and 𝑙 is the signed hyperbolic distance to the middle segment.
Then the 𝑙-lines {𝑙 =𝐶} are perpendicular to all the fibers, we glue along these 𝑙-lines to get an 1-Lipschitz map.

For the horocycle method, we contract to the middle line by gluing along the horocycles, note that the horocycles are perpendicular to all the lines between \a and \a′.

There are more gluing method, say taking curves in the picture, and gluing, this immediately gives a 3-cupsed sphere.

various coordinates

polar coordinates

Local expression: \d𝑠2 =\d\rh2 +sinh2⁡\rh\d\si2,
where \rh is the hyperbolic distance from 𝑝 to 𝑝0, and \si is the directed angle between some fixed direction and the geodesic [𝑝0,𝑝].

This model is used for (not only) cones.

horocyclic coordinates

Local expression: \d𝑠2 =\d\rh2 +𝑒2\rh\d𝑡2,
where \rh is the signed hyperbolic distance from 𝑝 to the horocycle ℎ, and 𝑡 is the unique value s.t. the geodesic [𝑝,ℎ(𝑡)] is perpendicular to ℎ.

This model is used for (not only) cusps.

Fermi coordinates

Local expression: \d𝑠2 =\d\rh2 +cosh2⁡\rh\d𝑡,
where \rh is the signed hyperbolic distance from 𝑝 to the line ℓ, and 𝑡 is the unique value s.t. the geodesic [𝑝,ℓ(𝑡)] is perpendicular to ℓ.

This model is used for (not only) funnels.

lambda length

definitions

Definition.

The lambda length for a geodesic line \g w.r.t. to horocycles is defined as \la(\g) =𝑒ℓ(\g)2, where ℓ(\g) is the signed distance between the horocycles.

(the sign is positive if the horocycles do not intersect, negative if intersect, zero if are tangent)

Another model to define the lambda length is the Minkowski space (hyperboloid model).

Consider \R2,1 with \d𝑠2 =\d𝑥2 +\d𝑦2 −\d𝑧2, define \H ={𝑥2 +𝑦2 −𝑧2 = −1,𝑧 >0}, there is a steoreographic projection identifying \Hc and \Dbb: for 𝑎 ∈\Hc, take the line connecting 𝑎 and (0,0, −1) and map 𝑎 to its intersection with \Dbb ⊂{𝑧 =0}.
Define \inpd, by \inpd𝑎,𝑏 =𝑎1𝑏1 +𝑎2𝑏2 −𝑎3𝑏3, then the hyperbolic distance is given by
𝑑(𝑎,𝑏) =\arccosh( −\inpd𝑎,𝑏).

Proof of the distance formula.

Consider 𝑎,𝑏 ∈\Hc, the corresponding images are 𝑥 =𝑎1𝑎3+1 +𝑖𝑎2𝑎3+1,𝑦 =𝑏1𝑏3+1 +𝑖𝑏2𝑏3+1 ∈\Dbb. Definition.

Define the positive light cone by 𝐿+ ={𝑎 ∈\R2,1|\inpd𝑎,𝑎 =0,𝑎3 >0}.
For any 𝑎 ∈𝐿+, it determines a horocycle ℎ(𝑎) :={𝑥 ∈\Hc|\inpd𝑎,𝑥 = −1√2}.
The lambda length for between horocycles ℎ(𝑎),ℎ(𝑏) is defined by \la𝑎𝑏 =√−\inpd𝑎,𝑏.

For the identification of the two definitions, see Lemma 4.1 in Penner.

an important relation

Proposition (Lemma 4.4 in Penner).

The hyperbolic length satisfies 𝐿𝑟 =\la𝑝𝑞\la𝑝𝑟\la𝑞𝑟.

This can be shown by direct calculation in the upper half plane model.

the Ptolemy's relations

Proposition (Ptolemy relation, Proposition 2.6(a) in Penner)

\la\a\la\g +\la\b\la\de =\la\th\la𝜂.

Proof (using the length relation).
Note that \la\de\la\a\la\th +\la\g\la\b\la\th =\la𝜂\la\a\la\b, we get the required identity.

Proof (direct proof with hyperboloid model).

Suppose these lambda lengths come from 𝑢1,⋯,𝑢4 ∈𝐿+, then the Ptolemy's relation reads \la12\la34+\la23\la14=\la13\la24.Up to some rescaling, we suppose 𝑢𝑖 are of the same height in 𝐿+, in other words, they lie on an Euclidean circle in 𝐿+ ⊂\R3.
